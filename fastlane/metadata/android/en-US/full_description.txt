Chess against a neural network that runs entirely on your phone. No server, no
account, no network permission — the app cannot talk to the internet at all.

Five difficulty levels are five separate Maia networks, each trained only on
games played by humans in one rating band: roughly 1100, 1300, 1500, 1700 and
1900 Elo.

That matters more than it sounds. A conventional engine playing badly plays
badly in a way no human does — three brilliant moves, then a rook hung for no
reason. Maia was trained to predict the move a human of that rating actually
played, mistakes included, so its errors are the ones you recognise from your
own games. There is no tree search at all: one pass through the network picks
the move, in a few milliseconds, on any phone.

The two strongest levels get a light safety check that rejects moves hanging a
piece outright. The three weakest levels deliberately do not — hanging pieces is
what chess at that rating looks like.

Design is Bauhaus by way of neo-brutalism, and it goes all the way down to the
pieces: the king is a cross, the queen an arch, the rook a square, the bishop a
diamond, the knight a triangle, the pawn a circle. No lettering, no colour
coding — the shape alone tells you what it is.

Play either colour. Move by dragging a piece or by tapping it and then its
destination. Undo takes back your move and the reply together.

Maia is the work of CSSLab at the University of Toronto. This app packages it
for Android; the research is theirs.
